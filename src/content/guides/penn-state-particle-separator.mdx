## 1. Why This Guide Exists

The Penn State Particle Separator (PSPS) is one of the least expensive, fastest diagnostic tools available on a commercial dairy — and one of the easiest to misuse. This guide is the reference I hand to farm teams and students who want to move past "run the sieve and write down the percentages" to actually using the tool to make feeding decisions.

<Callout title="Who This Guide Is For">
Farm managers, feeders, veterinarians, and nutrition students who want a working, field-ready understanding of particle-size assessment — not a laboratory methods paper.
</Callout>

## 2. Equipment and Setup

The current standard separator uses four fractions: a top screen (19 mm), a middle screen (8 mm), a fourth screen on newer models (4 mm), and a solid bottom pan. Before you sample:

- Set the separator on a flat, stable, level surface.
- Confirm the screens are clean and undamaged — bent screen mesh will distort every reading taken with that unit going forward.
- Sample size matters more than most people assume: 1.2–1.5 kg of as-fed material is the standard target. Too little sample gives noisy, unrepresentative percentages; too much overloads the screens and prevents proper separation.

## 3. Sampling Technique

Collect from at least five to eight locations across the feed bunk or mixer discharge, combining into one composite sample — a single grab sample from one spot is not representative of what the group is actually eating. For sorting diagnostics specifically, take a matched pair: fresh TMR immediately after delivery, and refusals from the same pen 4–6 hours later (or just before the next feeding).

## 4. Running the Separator

1. Add the composite sample to the top screen.
2. Shaking method matters and should be kept consistent between samples: the standard protocol is five lateral shakes, rotate 90°, five more shakes, repeat for a total of 40 shakes (eight cycles), holding the unit level throughout.
3. Weigh the material retained on each screen and the pan separately.
4. Calculate each fraction as a percentage of total recovered weight — not of the original sample weight, since some material loss during handling is normal and shouldn't be attributed to a nonexistent fraction.

<PracticalTakeaway>
Consistency in shaking technique matters as much as consistency in sampling. If more than one person runs the separator on your operation, agree on and write down the exact shake protocol you're using — comparing results across inconsistent shaking technique produces noise that looks like real variation.
</PracticalTakeaway>

## 5. Interpreting the Screens

### Typical Target Ranges — Lactating Cow TMR

| Screen | Typical Range | What It Reflects |
|---|---|---|
| Top (19 mm) | 2–8% | Long-fiber stimulation; oversupply risks sorting |
| Middle (8 mm) | 30–50% | Primary effective-fiber fraction |
| Fourth (4 mm), where present | 10–20% | Transitional fraction, forage- and grain-dependent |
| Bottom pan | 30–40% | Fines — forage and concentrate combined |

These ranges shift meaningfully with forage type (corn silage-based diets typically run lower on the top screen than grass hay-based diets), total ration NDF, and herd-specific tolerance — treat them as a starting reference, not a pass/fail threshold.

### Reading Corn Silage Specifically

For corn silage alone (not the full TMR), a well-processed silage with an adequate kernel processing score should show relatively little intact kernel material on the top and middle screens — visible whole or half kernels in the top-screen fraction usually indicate inadequate kernel processing at harvest, a separate issue from forage particle length itself and one that affects starch availability regardless of chop length.

## 6. Diagnosing Sorting

This is where the separator earns its keep beyond a one-time snapshot. Run the fresh-TMR-versus-refusals comparison described in Section 3, and compare top-screen percentages specifically:

- **Refusal top-screen % noticeably higher than fresh TMR top-screen %** → cows are sorting against long particles, consistent with classic feed-sorting behavior. Investigate TMR moisture (most sorting-prone diets run under ~45% moisture) and forage particle length.
- **Refusal bottom-pan % noticeably higher than fresh** → less common, but seen in some rations where cows leave fine, less palatable material (often associated with certain byproduct feeds or over-fine grain) — investigate palatability and ingredient inclusion rather than forage chop length.

## 7. Common Misreadings to Avoid

**Treating any single screen in isolation.** A target-range middle-screen percentage paired with an excessive top screen and a deficient bottom pan is not a "passing" result — read the full distribution together, and alongside total ration NDF and forage NDF source.

**Comparing across labs or across separator units without accounting for technique.** Screen wear, shake protocol, and even ambient humidity affecting sample moisture can shift results by several percentage points. Track trends over time from the same equipment and technique rather than chasing an exact published benchmark number.

**Assuming the separator measures digestibility or nutrient content.** It measures physical particle size only. A short-chopped, high-NDF forage and a short-chopped, low-NDF forage can produce nearly identical PSPS distributions while requiring very different ration strategies.

## 8. Putting It Together

Used correctly, the PSPS is a five-minute check that answers a specific, narrow question well: what is the physical particle-size structure of what's actually reaching — and actually being eaten by — the cow. Pair it with forage NDF and NDFd analysis, bunk and manure observation, and rumination data where available, and it becomes one of the highest-value, lowest-cost diagnostic habits on a well-run dairy.
