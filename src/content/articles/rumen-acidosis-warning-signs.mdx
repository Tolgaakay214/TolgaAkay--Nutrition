By the time bulk tank components drop and cull rates climb, subacute ruminal acidosis has usually been running in the background for weeks. SARA is a management disease more than a feed-ingredient disease — which is exactly why it's preventable, and why it's so often missed.

## What's Actually Happening in the Rumen

SARA is defined as ruminal pH depressed to roughly 5.5–5.8 for extended periods each day — not the dramatic acute acidosis crash of a grain overload event, but a chronic, repeated dip below the threshold where fiber-digesting bacteria function normally. Every time rapidly fermentable carbohydrate outpaces the rumen's buffering capacity — from saliva bicarbonate, from the diet's physically effective fiber, from rumen wall absorption — pH drops. A single dip isn't the problem. Cows that spend multiple hours a day below pH 5.8, day after day, are the ones that develop SARA.

<Callout title="Why It's Easy to Miss">
SARA rarely announces itself. There's no single dramatic symptom — instead, a cluster of quietly declining indicators: milk fat depression, inconsistent manure consistency across the pen, more sorted feed at the bunk, a creeping rise in lameness incidence weeks later. Each one, alone, has a dozen other possible causes.
</Callout>

## The Signs Worth Tracking Together

**Milk fat depression** is the single most useful herd-level signal, particularly a drop in fat without a proportional drop in protein — a pattern consistent with altered rumen biohydrogenation producing trans fatty acid isomers that directly suppress fat synthesis in the mammary gland.

**Manure consistency variation within a pen** — not just loose manure, but visible inconsistency cow-to-cow within the same group fed the same TMR — often reflects individual variation in sorting behavior and intake pattern more than it reflects the ration on paper.

**Feed sorting against long particles**, visible as a bunk that looks progressively finer as the day goes on, concentrates fermentable carbohydrate intake into fewer, larger boluses rather than the steady trickle the rumen buffering system is built to handle.

**Rumination time**, where herds have collar or ear-tag monitoring, is a leading indicator — falling rumination often precedes the milk fat and manure changes by days.

## Where Prevention Actually Lives

SARA prevention is overwhelmingly a physically effective fiber and delivery-consistency problem, not primarily an ingredient-selection problem. The starch and sugar content of a ration matters, but a well-formulated ration delivered inconsistently — variable mix times, inconsistent TMR moisture, bunk management that lets cows sort — will still produce SARA in a meaningful share of the herd.

<PracticalTakeaway>
Run a Penn State Particle Separator check on fresh TMR and on refusals from the same pen, same day. A meaningfully finer particle-size distribution in the refusals than in the fresh mix is direct evidence of sorting, and sorting is one of the most common, most fixable root causes of herd-level SARA.
</PracticalTakeaway>

## A Feeding-Management Checklist

Getting ahead of SARA usually comes down to: verifying forage NDF and effective fiber actually match the formulated ration (not book values), checking TMR moisture and mix consistency at the bunk rather than only on paper, confirming feed is pushed up frequently enough that cows aren't gorging after a period of empty bunk, and watching bunk space and stocking density — overcrowded pens increase the meal-size and slug-feeding behavior that drives acidosis risk independent of ration formulation.

None of this requires a new feed additive. It requires treating the rumen as the fermentation vat it is, and managing the rate and consistency of what enters it — which is squarely a feeding-management discipline, not a nutrition-formulation one.
