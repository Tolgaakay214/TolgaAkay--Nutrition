Every transition cow moves through the same physiological cliff edge: at calving, calcium demand for colostrum and milk production jumps several-fold in a matter of hours, while the cow's own calcium-mobilizing machinery is still catching up. Dietary Cation-Anion Difference — DCAD — is one of the most effective levers we have to make that transition safer, and one of the most frequently miscalculated.

## What DCAD Actually Measures

DCAD is a simple idea expressed as a slightly intimidating number. It quantifies the balance between the strong cations (sodium, potassium) and strong anions (chloride, sulfur) in the diet:

```
DCAD (meq/kg DM) = (Na/0.023 + K/0.039) − (Cl/0.0355 + S/0.016)
```

A positive, high DCAD diet (typical of a lactating ration, roughly +150 to +450 meq/kg) tends to raise blood pH slightly — a mild metabolic alkalosis. A negative DCAD diet (typical of a well-designed close-up ration, roughly −50 to −150 meq/kg) induces a mild, controlled metabolic acidosis.

<Callout title="Why This Matters">
That controlled acidosis is the entire point. A mildly acidotic cow upregulates parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptor sensitivity in bone and kidney, meaning she mobilizes and reabsorbs calcium far more efficiently the moment she needs it — at calving.
</Callout>

## The Physiology in Practice

In a cow fed a high-DCAD, alkaline diet through the close-up period — which describes most diets built from typical high-potassium forages without adjustment — PTH receptors are effectively desensitized. When the calcium demand of calving hits, she cannot mobilize skeletal calcium or reabsorb it from the kidney fast enough. The result, on a spectrum, is clinical milk fever (down cow, unable to rise) at the severe end, and subclinical hypocalcemia — a much larger and often invisible problem — affecting a much greater share of the herd.

Subclinical hypocalcemia doesn't put a cow down, but it measurably increases the incidence of retained placenta, displaced abomasum, metritis, and reduced early-lactation dry matter intake. This is why DCAD management earns its place as a foundational piece of transition cow programs, not an optional add-on.

## Getting the Close-Up Diet Right

Two variables drive most DCAD program failures, in my experience:

**Forage potassium is almost always underestimated.** Many close-up forages — particularly grass hays and some grass silages — run 2.5–3.5% K on a DM basis, well above what most base rations assume. Since potassium is the dominant cation pulling DCAD positive, an unmeasured high-K forage can quietly defeat an otherwise well-designed anionic supplementation program.

**Urine pH is the verification step, not the DCAD number itself.** DCAD on paper tells you what should happen. Urine pH tells you what is happening in that specific group of cows, with that specific water source and forage lot, right now. Target urine pH for Holsteins on an appropriately negative DCAD diet is generally 6.0–6.8; Jerseys typically run slightly lower. If urine pH sits above 7.0 despite a calculated negative DCAD, something in your actual forage mineral content or anion product intake doesn't match your spreadsheet.

<PracticalTakeaway>
Before troubleshooting a DCAD program, get a current wet chemistry mineral analysis on the actual close-up forage lot in the bunker or bag — not a book value, not last year's analysis. Then verify the program is working with urine pH sampling on a representative subset of the close-up group, 5–7 days into the diet, sampled mid-day.
</PracticalTakeaway>

## A Common Mistake

The most frequent error I see is treating DCAD as a single number to hit rather than a system to manage. A ration that calculates to −120 meq/kg on paper but is fed inconsistently, mixed into a TMR with sorting issues, or delivered to cows still eating residual bunk feed from the far-off group, will not produce the intended physiological response. DCAD management is a program — accurate forage testing, correct anion salt inclusion, adequate magnesium (which is required as a cofactor for PTH function and is often the silent limiting factor), and verification through urine pH — not a formula you calculate once and forget.

Get the fundamentals right here, and the downstream metabolic disease conversation — displaced abomasum, ketosis, retained placenta — gets meaningfully easier before you've addressed any of them directly.
