A Penn State Particle Separator (PSPS) reading is one of the most useful five-minute diagnostics available on a dairy — and one of the most frequently over-interpreted. The numbers on the screens tell you a distribution, not a verdict. Reading them correctly means knowing what they can and can't tell you.

## What the Screens Are Actually Sorting

The current four-screen (three-sieve-plus-pan) separator sorts particles by size into fractions typically reported as: the top screen (19 mm — long, coarse forage particles), the middle screen (8 mm), a fourth screen on newer models (4 mm), and the bottom pan (fine particles, under roughly 1.18 mm). This is a physical sieve, not a nutrient analysis — it tells you nothing directly about NDF digestibility, starch content, or fermentation quality. It tells you the physical particle-size structure of what's actually in front of the cow.

<Callout title="A Common Misreading">
A "good" middle-screen percentage in isolation means very little. The same 40% middle-screen reading can represent a well-balanced ration in one herd and a ration critically short of effective fiber in another, depending on forage type, total ration NDF, and what's happening on the top screen and bottom pan alongside it.
</Callout>

## Reading the Distribution as a Whole

**Top screen (19 mm):** This fraction stimulates rumination and saliva buffering, but too much of it — particularly with long, dry hay or under-processed straw — is exactly what invites sorting. Typical TMR guidelines run roughly 2–8% on this screen depending on forage source; corn silage-based diets generally sit at the lower end.

**Middle screen (8 mm):** Usually the largest single fraction, generally 30–50% of a well-balanced lactating TMR, representing well-chopped forage particles that are still long enough to support rumen mat formation without being sortable the way top-screen material is.

**Bottom pan (fine particles):** This is where readings get misread most often. A high bottom-pan percentage is frequently blamed entirely on forage chop length, when in a large share of cases it's driven by grain and concentrate particle size, or by high-moisture ingredients breaking down further during mixing. Target ranges of roughly 30–40% are typical, but the composition of that fraction — forage fines versus concentrate — matters more than the number alone.

## The Comparison That Actually Diagnoses Sorting

A single PSPS reading on fresh TMR is a snapshot. The diagnostically useful comparison is fresh TMR versus bunk refusals, or morning versus late-afternoon samples from the same load. A meaningful shift toward finer particles later in the feeding cycle is direct, quantifiable evidence of selective sorting — cows eating the fine, energy-dense fraction first and leaving the long, less palatable material behind.

<PracticalTakeaway>
Sample fresh TMR and 4–6 hour refusals from the same pen on the same day, run both through the separator, and compare the top-screen percentage specifically. A drop of more than a few percentage points in the refusal's top-screen fraction versus the fresh mix indicates sorting severe enough to warrant investigating TMR moisture, mix time, and forage particle length together — not sequentially.
</PracticalTakeaway>

## Where Judgment Still Matters

Screen percentages are a starting point for a conversation, not an automatic ration adjustment. Two herds can post nearly identical PSPS numbers and need opposite interventions, depending on forage moisture, whether cows are showing rumination and manure signs consistent with adequate effective fiber, and what's actually happening to milk fat test. Treat the separator as what it is: the fastest, cheapest window you have into what's physically reaching the cow — always read alongside forage analysis, bunk observation, and cow-side signals, never in isolation.
